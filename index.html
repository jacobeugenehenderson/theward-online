A living model of a neighborhood

The real ground, the real sky, and the people who make a place out of it. Not a map of somewhere — a version of it you can be in.

It begins as a survey. The actual streets, the actual buildings, the actual trees, drawn from public record and measured against photographs of the ground. Then it is given the neighborhood’s own weather and its own hour. Then it is handed to the people who live there, and they fill it.

Two things at once, and the name has always held both. A ward is a civic division. A ward is someone in your care. And the ward of a castle is the wall that keeps it. Community building and neighborhood fortification are not two ambitions that need reconciling; they are one old word, and it was already on the door.

The instruments

The weather overhead is the weather outside

The Ward reads the live conditions for the ground it stands on and shows what is happening now — the overcast that arrived this afternoon, golden hour at the true minute, the first snow as it falls. The sun and moon sit where they actually are, and the moon shows its actual phase. Look up at night and the stars are in their real places, each in the colour it really is.

The trees are the ones that are actually there

Not scenery. The species a neighborhood really has, placed where the city’s own inventory says they stand, at the heights they actually reach — a sugar maple comes up short of a linden because it does. Bark is photographed rather than invented, and the leaves know what season it is.

Every building opens

A directory of the whole neighborhood, and behind every address a card. The places someone has claimed carry photographs, hours, a menu, what is on this week. The ones nobody has claimed still open — the Ward writes their card from the building’s own record: when it was built, how it is built, what it was for. Nothing in the neighborhood is a dead pixel.

The Almanac

Time and temperature, the hour, the moon — and a slider, so you can look at the evening before you get to it.

The Bulletin Board

Buy Nothing, services, notices, missed connections. Anyone recognized can post; some corners are anonymous by default, because some things need to be said that way.

The ticker tells you who is open late what is on tonight where the band is playing which kitchen is still cooking what the neighbours are giving away

Open businesses glow. The light is the light outside, and the moon is the moon you would see.

None of this is scheduled content, and none of it was written this morning. It is the neighborhood reporting itself — a place is alive or it is not, and the map should be able to tell you which.

There is no account

No email, no password, no name. Your device keeps a small anonymous marker, and that is the whole of it.

Standing in the Ward is a matter of where you actually are. Three of the four ways in are a single act at a single place, because being there is the proof. Only one asks you to come back, and it is the one with no address to check.

1

Townie — you are aroundA townie can live anywhere, so no one place can vouch for them. Scan the code at a place in the neighborhood on three separate days inside a fortnight and you are recognized: you can review places, post to the board, and answer a neighbour’s notice. Not three scans — three days. The point is that you keep turning up.

2

Resident — you live in this buildingScan your building’s own code and you are in, with a neighbour already verified or a device you have linked as the other ways round. That opens your building’s private lobby, and it lasts a year.

3

Guardian — this place is yours to look afterThe code posted at the place makes your phone its editor. Hours, announcements, photographs, what is on the menu tonight. No login to lose and no paperwork to file.

4

Keyholder — you work hereA guardian hands you exactly the keys you need and no others — the menu, or the photographs, or the hours. Replies go out in the business’s voice, never a person’s, so whoever is on shift answers as the place itself.

Every one of them is a townie from the moment they are verified. Stewardship, not ownership — access belongs to a device, and a device can be handed back.

A list of residents cannot be produced, because the information to build one is never collected. That is a property of how it is built, not a policy that could be revised later.

Except, deliberately. Couriers are verified — identity, and a background check. Someone who will be at a stranger’s door is a different question from someone reading a notice board, and the two are not run on the same terms. Saying so plainly is what makes the paragraph above worth anything.

A delivery service the neighborhood owns

Couriers are neighbours. Restaurants keep the whole of the food price. Most of the surcharge goes to the person who carried it, and the platform keeps only what it costs to run.

The design principle underneath is one rule applied everywhere: keep designing away the need for the dangerous thing, rather than managing it. A courier says when they are available and orders arrive into confirmed capacity, so there is never an orphaned order and never a reason to pressure anyone into taking one. Declining costs nothing. You are accountable only for what you chose to carry. Trips are short and comparable, so there is nothing to cherry-pick and nothing to police.

Reliability comes from belonging — neighbours with standing — not from penalties. That turns out to be the same thing as treating people properly, which is not a coincidence.

Carry for your neighborhood

Sixteen and over to carry on foot or by bike; eighteen to drive. You say when you are available and you accept what you choose to accept — nothing is assigned to you, and turning something down costs you nothing at all.

Couriers are the one part of the Ward that is verified, because you will be at someone’s door. That is a deliberate exception, not an oversight.

Tell me when couriers open

Not open yet. Couriers open neighborhood by neighborhood, once there are places to carry from.

The ground arrives on its own. The knowledge does not.

Streets, buildings, terrain and trees come out of public record. Which corner matters, what the building used to be, who to ask — that is not in any dataset, and a neighborhood is the only place it exists.

That is what a host brings, and it is why a host is a partner rather than a customer. Nobody else can supply the layer that makes it a place.

Ask about hosting a Ward

You start with a ZIP. You end with a neighborhood.

Not the one a bureaucrat drew — the one people actually mean. You frame it on an aerial photograph, click the streets that bound it and watch the ring close, loop out the strip that was never really part of it, wave in the one building that gives the place its character. Then one button.

Everything below either arrives on its own or comes out of a public portal. There is no Google in it, no tile vendor, and nothing licensed from anybody.
